Why You’re Tired When Sleep Isn’t Fixing It

Energy is not one account. A low reading is a question, not a verdict.

By
MO
Date
October 19, 2026
Category
Energy & Capacity

To the person who is tired in a way that sleep is not fixing.

You probably know the feeling. You sleep, and you wake up tired. You take time off, and a few days after you come back you feel exactly as you did before. You do the sensible things. The work still gets done, and the output is acceptable. But the person doing it is running on something thinner than before.

When that happens, most of us reach for the same explanation: I must be weak, or lazy, or simply in the wrong life. Book 1 of the GoBeyond Series, Energy, offers a different explanation. It is closer to an accounting error. And accounting errors can be fixed.

The one-account myth

Most of us treat energy as a single tank. When it reads low, we fill it the only way we know how: sleep, rest, a break.

The trouble is that the tank is not single. As the book puts it: “Energy is not one account. It is at least nine.” Each one is filled by something different and drained by something different. And one can be completely full while another sits empty.

That is why rest so often fails to do what we expect. “You can sleep nine hours and be useless because your attention is shredded.” You can be rested, fit and sharp, while something further down has quietly gone dry. Because we only measure the account we know how to measure, the emptiness in the others goes unnoticed until it shows up as something else.

Here is the shift that changes everything: “If you have one account, every low reading has one answer: rest more. If you have nine, a low reading is a question.” Which account is low, and what actually fills it?

The nine accounts, briefly

  • Physical is the base: sleep, movement, nourishment and recovery. It sets a ceiling on everything else.
  • Mental is your capacity for concentrated thought. It drains through decisions and constant switching, and it recovers through uninterrupted blocks of time.
  • Emotional is your capacity to stay present without becoming brittle or withdrawn. Difficult conversations and disappointment spend it.
  • Relational is unusual, because people both fill it and drain it.
  • Creative is the capacity to connect things that were not connected before. It is restored by novelty, play and unstructured time, which are the first things cut when a schedule tightens.
  • Security is how settled you are about your material circumstances. It is not about how much you have.
  • Spiritual is the sense that your work is connected to something larger than itself.

The book names others as well, but the point is not the exact count. The point is the move from one to many, because it changes what you do when you feel low. “A person who is creatively empty does not need more sleep. They need three hours nobody is allowed to book.”

Not every drain is a problem

Once you start looking, it is tempting to treat every drain as an enemy. The book is careful here: “Not everything that drains you should be removed.”

Some drains are necessary. They cost you, and something comes back: a person helped to grow, a problem solved, a relationship deepened. Caring for a small child, supporting a friend in a hard season, looking after an ageing parent: these cost a great deal, and they should. Necessary drains are not eliminated. They are paced, and you recover from them.

Other drains are unnecessary. They cost and return nothing proportionate. They are rarely dramatic. The recurring meeting that made sense two years ago. The report nobody reads. The obligation you accepted when your life had a different shape.

How did they get there? “Most unnecessary drains arrived for a good reason that has since expired.” And they stay because nothing in an ordinary week ever prompts you to look at them again. “They are added one at a time and removed never.”

The drain test: one action for this week

Pick one recurring commitment, one you suspect costs more than it gives.

Then ask a single question: if I stopped this entirely, what would I actually lose?

There are three honest answers.

Nothing significant. Then it is an unnecessary drain, and it should go. Not reduced or tidied up. Stopped.

Something real. Then it is a necessary drain. Keep it, and manage it on purpose: pace it, recover from it, and stop apologising to yourself for the fact that it costs.

Something I’d rather not admit. This is often the most useful answer. Some drains persist because they give us something we would prefer not to name: the feeling of being busy, a way to avoid harder work, the reassurance of being needed. Naming it is uncomfortable. It is also usually the moment the drain becomes removable.

If your answer is the first one, there is one more step, and it matters as much as the cut. Decide in advance what the freed hour is for. The book is blunt about what happens otherwise: a cleared hour tends to be claimed within about three weeks, usually by something that arrived while you were not looking and was never examined. Removing a drain does not hand you a useful hour. It gives you an hour you now have the capacity to use, but only if you have decided what to use it on.

The deepest account

There is one form of tiredness that does not respond to sleep, exercise, time off or anything else on this list. The work continues and the competence remains, but the meaning has gone. People describe it as flatness. They are not sad or underslept. They are doing acceptable work in a life whose point they can no longer feel.

Energy is plain about what its author believes fills that account: connection with God, through prayer, Scripture and the ordinary practice of paying attention to Him. Not prayer as a list of requests, but unhurried quiet where nothing is being asked for and something might be received. Scripture read not as information but as a place where something might be said to you in the season you are in.

If you don’t share that faith, the invitation still stands. Take this account seriously and find out what fills it. The alternative is to leave it unmanaged and misread its emptiness as a verdict on your life.

Where to start

You are not tired because you are weak. You are tired because you have been spending from many accounts and replenishing only a few. Start with one honest question about one commitment, and see what comes back.
