Settlement explained

Settlement, explained.

Six concepts that account for most of the confusion in cross-border payments. Each one is explained on its own terms, before any argument is made about what should be built.

What settlement means

Settlement is the discharge of an obligation between two parties by an actual transfer of value. When a bank in one country owes value to a bank in another, that obligation is settled when value moves between accounts the two institutions can each recognise as theirs — typically accounts held with each other, with a shared correspondent, or with a central bank.

Everything else in a payment — the instruction, the compliance checks, the conversion, the statement entry the customer eventually sees — surrounds that moment. Confusing the surrounding activity with settlement itself is the most common error in market commentary, and it makes systems look like competitors when they are in fact doing different jobs.

Clearing versus settlement

Clearing is the transmission, reconciliation and confirmation of instructions, and the calculation of the resulting obligations. Settlement is the transfer of value that discharges them.

A clearing process may net many instructions into a single obligation per participant per cycle, which reduces the value that must actually move. Settlement then happens once, for the net figure, in central bank money or another agreed settlement asset. A real-time gross settlement system takes the opposite approach: each instruction settles individually and immediately, removing netting risk at the cost of requiring more liquidity.

Neither approach is better in the abstract. They trade liquidity efficiency against risk, and different systems choose different points on that trade.

Correspondent banking

A bank that has no presence in a market reaches it through a bank that does. The local bank holds an account for the foreign bank, and value moves between institutions by adjusting the balances on those accounts. The account the foreign bank holds abroad is its nostro; the same account, seen from the local bank’s side, is a vostro.

The model works, and it carries an enormous share of world trade. Its costs are structural rather than incidental: each institution in a chain applies its own compliance requirements, holds its own liquidity, charges its own fees and provides its own cut-off times, and the originating customer sees the sum of all of them.

Since the 2010s the number of correspondent relationships has declined in several regions as banks reassessed the risk and cost of maintaining them — a trend documented in work by the Financial Stability Board and the Bank for International Settlements. The consequence for affected corridors is fewer routes, not necessarily worse ones. [INSERT VERIFIED FIGURE — de-risking trend data, with citation]

FX and liquidity

A cross-border payment that changes currency requires someone to hold both currencies and to be willing to exchange them at a price, at a size, at a moment. That party takes a position and is compensated for it.

Two things are often conflated here. Price is the rate obtained relative to a reference rate, plus any margin. Availability is whether the currency can be obtained at all, at the size and time needed. In several African corridors, availability rather than price has been the binding constraint for businesses at various times — which is why a narrative built only around “spreads” misdescribes the problem. No settlement design removes the need for someone to hold and price currency risk. A design can change who holds it, how it is priced, and how visible that price is. It cannot make it free, and any claim that it does should be treated as a red flag.

Settlement finality

Finality is the point after which a transfer cannot be revoked — not by the sender, not by the insolvency of a participant, and not by the system operator. It is a legal property as much as a technical one, defined by the rules of the system and by the law governing it.

This matters commercially. A payment that appears in a beneficiary’s account but is not final can still be reversed, and a business that releases goods against it is exposed. When assessing any settlement proposal, the question is not how fast the balance updates but at what point, under whose rules and under which law, the transfer became irrevocable.

Stable-value settlement models

A recurring proposal is to settle between institutions using a stable-value instrument — typically a token intended to hold a constant value against a reference currency — rather than through correspondent balances.

The arguments in favour are that transfers can be effected outside domestic banking hours and that the record of transfer is shared rather than reconciled. The arguments against are that the instrument introduces its own credit, custody, redemption and legal risks; that finality depends on the legal treatment of the instrument in each relevant jurisdiction; and that somebody must still convert to and from local currency at each end, which is where much of the cost sits.

Regulatory treatment differs sharply by jurisdiction and continues to change. Any claim that a stable-value model removes foreign exchange cost, eliminates spread or guarantees value stability is not supported by the mechanics: conversion still happens, and the party performing it is still compensated.

Eko Infrastructure does not take the position that stable-value settlement is either the answer or a distraction. It is one design among several, and its suitability is corridor-specific, regulator-specific and legally contingent. [CONFIRM — current regulatory treatment by jurisdiction, with citation and date]

Corridor intelligence

Corridor intelligence.

A corridor is not a market size. It is a set of named participants moving specific value under specific rules, and it can be described precisely if you ask the right questions in the right order.

The method

Most corridor analysis begins with a total addressable market and works down. That produces a number that cannot be acted on. Starting from the participants and working up produces a description that can be tested against reality.

1. Participants

Who actually moves value on this corridor today? Name the banks, the payment institutions, the switches, the aggregators and the informal channels. If a corridor cannot be described in named institutions, the analysis is not yet real.

2. Flows

Separate the flows that get bundled together: trade payments for goods, trade payments for services, intra-group treasury movement, personal remittance, and government or institutional transfers. They have different values, different frequencies, different documentation and different regulatory treatment. A statistic about one is not evidence about another.

3. The cost stack

Cost is rarely a single fee. It is an origination fee, a conversion margin, intermediary charges, a receiving charge, and a set of costs that never appear on an invoice: working capital held against uncertainty, staff time spent reconciling, and delay in releasing goods. The visible fee is often the smaller half.

4. Friction points

Where does a transaction on this corridor actually stop? Common answers include compliance holds on incomplete party data, currency unavailable at the size required, cut-off times missed across time zones, beneficiary details that fail validation at the last hop, and reference data lost between intermediaries.

5. The last mile

How does value reach the beneficiary’s account domestically, through which licensed institution, on which rails, within which cut-off? Corridor propositions fail here more often than at settlement, and the last mile is the part that cannot be built remotely.

6. The regulatory perimeter

Which activities on this corridor require authorisation, in which market, and who holds it? Which reporting obligations attach, and to whom? A corridor design that is elegant and unlicensable is not a design.

Policy and regulation

Policy and regulation.

The regulatory architecture around cross-border payments, described as it is rather than as builders would prefer it to be. Regulation is the constraint that makes settlement infrastructure trustworthy, not an obstacle to route around.

Regulatory sandboxes

A sandbox is a supervised testing arrangement. A regulator permits a firm to test a defined proposition with a defined participant group, within limits — value caps, volume caps, a duration — and subject to reporting. The regulator retains the power to stop the test.

Four things a sandbox is not, each of which is regularly misrepresented in market communications:

  • It is not a commercial operating licence.
  • It is not permission to serve the general public.
  • It is not an endorsement, approval or recommendation of the firm by the regulator.
  • It is not a guarantee that authorisation will follow.

The distinction between applying to a sandbox, being admitted to one, and holding a licence is the single most commonly blurred line in this sector. A firm that has applied has done exactly that and nothing more.

Licensing categories

Payment activity is not one licence. Different jurisdictions define different categories, and an entity commonly needs several, or needs partners who hold them. Categories that recur across African markets include payment service provider or payment solution service provider licences; international money transfer operator authorisations; switching and processing licences; e-money issuance; and banking licences.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the question “are they licensed?” is incomplete without “for what activity, in which market?”. Second, an infrastructure layer that does not itself hold funds or convert currency may sit outside some categories entirely — but only if the boundary is genuinely maintained in the design, not merely in the marketing.

AML and CFT expectations

The baseline is set internationally by the FATF Recommendations and implemented by national law and regulator guidance. In practice that means a documented risk assessment; customer and business due diligence proportionate to risk; sanctions screening; ongoing transaction monitoring; suspicious activity reporting; record keeping; a named responsible officer; training; and independent testing.

For cross-border payments the requirement that most often bites is the transmission of complete originator and beneficiary information with the payment. Chains that lose that data create obligations for every downstream institution and produce the compliance holds that customers experience as unexplained delay.

Data protection

Payment data is personal data. Nigeria’s Data Protection Act 2023 established a statutory regime and a commission with supervisory powers; other markets in the region have their own regimes at varying stages of implementation, and European or UK rules may also apply depending on where an entity or its processors are established.

For corridor design this affects lawful basis, minimisation, retention, cross-border transfer mechanisms and the handling of data subject rights — and it affects them in both markets of the corridor simultaneously, which is the part most often underestimated.

Regional and continental policy

Continental initiatives shape what is possible at corridor level. The African Continental Free Trade Area addresses trade rules; the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System addresses payment and settlement between participating institutions in local currencies; regional economic communities pursue their own integration programmes.

These are not marketing backdrops. They determine which routes exist, which currencies can be settled in, and which institutions can reach each other — and a corridor proposition that does not engage with them seriously is unlikely to survive contact with a supervisor.

Technology

Technology.

Standards, architecture and evidence. The engineering questions that determine whether a settlement design can actually be supervised.

Messaging standards

Payment messages carry more than an amount. They carry the parties, their identifiers, the purpose, the charges and the references that let a beneficiary work out what the payment is for. When those fields are lost or truncated between systems, the consequences appear later as compliance holds and manual reconciliation.

ISO 20022 is the standard the industry has converged on, and its value is precisely that it carries structured, extensible party and remittance data rather than free text. Migration across markets and systems has been staged over years, and coexistence with older formats is the normal condition rather than an exception.

For anyone designing a corridor product, the practical question is not whether to support ISO 20022 but which fields survive each hop on the actual route, and what happens to a payment when one of them does not. [CONFIRM — current migration status by market and system, with source]

Compliance architecture

Compliance is an architectural property, not a department. Three design decisions determine whether a system can be supervised.

  • Where controls run. Controls enforced before routing prevent bad instructions from entering the chain. Controls applied after the fact produce reports about payments that already left.
  • What is recorded. Recording an outcome is not enough. Recording the rule version, the input data and the decision-maker is what allows a supervisor to ask about a past date and get an answer.
  • Who owns the decision. In a chain of institutions, exactly one party must own each regulated decision. Designs that leave this ambiguous fail their first serious review.

Transaction monitoring

Monitoring compares activity against an expected pattern and raises alerts on deviation. Its engineering difficulties are well known: rules produce large numbers of false positives; thresholds that are too tight bury analysts and too loose miss activity; and tuning without versioning destroys the ability to explain past behaviour.

Two claims should always be treated sceptically. First, that a monitoring system detects all illicit activity — none does. Second, that machine learning removes the need for explainability — supervisors ask why an alert was or was not raised, and a model that cannot answer creates a regulatory problem rather than solving one.

Auditability

An auditable system can answer, for any transaction and any date: what was known, what rule applied, who decided, when, and what happened next. That requires append-only records, versioned rules, time stamps from a trusted source, and retention for the longest applicable period across the markets involved.

Auditability is also what makes disputes tractable. When two institutions disagree about a payment, the party that can reconstruct the sequence with evidence resolves the matter; the party that cannot absorbs the loss.

What distributed ledger designs change

Shared-ledger architectures change one thing genuinely: participants can read from a common record rather than reconciling separate ones. That is a real reduction in a real cost.

Several things they do not change. Somebody must still convert currency and be compensated for it. Somebody must still hold customer funds under a licence. Legal finality still depends on the law and the system rules, not on the data structure. Compliance obligations still attach to the institutions in the chain. And the last mile still runs through a licensed institution on domestic rails.

Eko Infrastructure takes no position for or against these designs. The useful question is always which of the five payment functions a proposal addresses, and what it leaves untouched.

Data sovereignty

Several markets have introduced requirements about where payment and personal data is stored and processed, and about the conditions for transferring it abroad. For corridor designs this is not a hosting preference; it determines architecture, because both markets’ requirements apply at once and can conflict.

The practical questions are: which data must remain in-market, which may be transferred under which mechanism, who can access it from where, and how that is evidenced. [CONFIRM — current residency requirements by market, with source and date]

Reference

Glossary.

Terms that get used loosely, defined precisely. Where a term is contested or used differently across jurisdictions, we say so rather than picking the convenient meaning.

ACDFI KLNOP RSTV

A

AML
Anti-money laundering. The body of law, regulation and controls intended to prevent the financial system being used to launder the proceeds of crime.
Authorised dealer
An institution permitted by a central bank to deal in foreign exchange. The term and its scope vary by jurisdiction.

C

CFT
Counter-financing of terrorism. Usually paired with AML in regulation and in a firm’s programme.
Clearing
The transmission, reconciliation and confirmation of payment instructions between institutions, and the calculation of the resulting obligations. Distinct from settlement.
Correspondent banking
An arrangement in which one bank holds an account for another so the second can reach a market where it has no presence.
Corridor
A directional pair of markets between which value moves — for example payments from market A to market B. A corridor has its own participants, rules, costs and frictions, and behaves differently in each direction.
Cut-off time
The time after which an instruction will be processed on the following business day in a given system or market. Cut-offs across time zones are a common and under-appreciated source of delay.

D

De-risking
The withdrawal of correspondent or customer relationships by institutions judging the compliance cost or risk to be too high, rather than in response to a specific finding.
Due diligence
The verification and risk assessment a regulated institution performs on a customer or counterparty, at onboarding and on an ongoing basis.

F

Finality
The point at which a transfer of value becomes irrevocable and unconditional. Defined by system rules and by governing law, not by how quickly a balance updates on a screen.
FX spread
The difference between the rate at which a party will buy a currency and the rate at which it will sell, or between the rate given to a customer and a reference rate. Compensation for holding and pricing currency risk. It can be moved or made visible; it cannot be conjured away.

I

Idempotency
A property of an operation such that repeating it produces no additional effect. Essential in payments, where a retried instruction must never create a second payment.
Interoperability
The ability of separate systems to work together to complete a transaction end to end, including the data, rules and legal arrangements that make that possible — not only a technical connection.
ISO 20022
An international standard for financial messaging that carries structured party, purpose and remittance data. Widely adopted, with staged migration across markets.

K

KYB
Know your business. Verification of a business counterparty: legal existence, ownership, control and the nature of its activity.
KYC
Know your customer. Verification of a customer’s identity and risk profile.

L

Last mile
The final leg of a cross-border payment, in which a licensed institution credits the beneficiary through domestic rails. Frequently where corridor propositions fail.
Liquidity provider
A party willing to hold and exchange currencies at a quoted price and size, taking a position in return for compensation.

N

Netting
Offsetting obligations between parties so that only the net amount is settled. Reduces the value that must move, at the cost of concentrating exposure until settlement occurs.
Nostro / vostro
Two views of the same correspondent account. Nostro is “our account with you”; vostro is “your account with us”.

O

Orchestration
Coordinating the sequence of dependent actions performed by different parties in a transaction, and holding its state until every leg completes or is unwound. Orchestration is not settlement.

P

PAPSS
The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System, developed with Afreximbank and African central banks to enable payments between participating institutions in local currencies. Full explainer.
Payout institution
The licensed institution in the receiving market that credits the beneficiary.
PSP
Payment service provider. A licensing category whose exact scope varies by jurisdiction.

R

Regulatory sandbox
A supervised arrangement permitting a defined test with defined participants, within limits and subject to reporting. Not a licence, and not an endorsement.
RTGS
Real-time gross settlement. A system in which each instruction settles individually and immediately, typically in central bank money, removing netting risk at the cost of higher liquidity requirements.

S

Sanctions screening
Checking parties and payment data against applicable sanctions and watchlists at onboarding and at transaction time.
Settlement
The transfer of value that discharges an obligation between parties. Distinct from messaging, clearing, conversion and payout.
Settlement asset
The instrument in which settlement occurs — central bank money, commercial bank money, or another asset agreed by the parties and permitted by the applicable rules.
Stablecoin
A token intended to hold a constant value against a reference asset, usually a currency. Regulatory treatment differs sharply by jurisdiction and continues to change. A stablecoin does not remove the need for currency conversion at either end of a corridor.
Switch
Domestic infrastructure routing transactions between institutions within a market, often operating the domestic card and transfer schemes.
SWIFT
A financial messaging network used by institutions to exchange standardised instructions. It is not itself clearing, conversion or settlement. Full explainer.

T

Transaction monitoring
Ongoing analysis of activity against expected patterns and rules, raising alerts for review. No monitoring programme detects all illicit activity.
Travel rule
The requirement that specified originator and beneficiary information accompanies a transfer, so that each institution in the chain receives complete data.

V

Velocity limit
A cap on the frequency or cumulative value of transactions over a period, applied per customer, beneficiary or corridor as a control.

Suggest a definition or a correction: media@ekoinfrastructure.com. Definitions are reviewed on the cycle described in our editorial standards.

Sourcing rules

Six rules we hold ourselves to.

Rule 01

Primary source or nothing

We cite the body that produced the data, not an article about it. Where only a secondary source exists, we say so.

Date everything

Every figure carries the period it covers and the date we retrieved it. Payment data ages quickly.

Definitions travel with numbers

What was counted, and how, is published alongside the figure. Most disagreements are definitional.

Estimates are labelled

Where we model, we show the inputs, assumptions and arithmetic, and call the output an estimate.

No superlatives without a dataset

“Largest”, “fastest growing” and multiples require a named dataset, a year and a definition.

Rule 06

Placeholders, not guesses

Where a figure is required but unverified, we publish a visible placeholder rather than an approximation.

Settlement · Explainer

Clearing versus settlement: the distinction that explains most cross-border confusion

Messaging, clearing, conversion, settlement and payout are five different things, performed by different parties, under different rules. Most disagreements about what a payment system “does” dissolve once they are separated.

By Eko Infrastructure editorial team Published [DATE] Last updated [DATE] 12 min read Source-checked

Ask five people in a payments conversation what happens when money moves across a border and you will get five answers that are each partly right. That is not carelessness. It is because the phrase “a payment” bundles together at least five distinct functions, and different specialists have different ones in mind.

Separating them is not pedantry. It determines which system is relevant to a given problem, which institution bears which risk, and whether a proposed improvement addresses the actual constraint or merely relocates it.

The five functions

Messaging is the transmission of a standardised instruction between institutions. It says who is paying whom, how much, in what currency, for what purpose, with what references. Messaging moves information, not value.

Clearing is the reconciliation and confirmation of those instructions and the calculation of the resulting obligations. It establishes who owes what to whom. Clearing may net many instructions into a single obligation per participant per cycle.

Conversion is the exchange of one currency for another at a price, performed by a party who holds both and takes a position. Conversion has a cost because holding and pricing currency risk has a cost.

Settlement is the transfer of value that discharges the obligation, in an agreed settlement asset, between accounts the institutions recognise. Settlement is the moment the obligation is extinguished.

Payout is the domestic credit to the beneficiary’s account, performed by a licensed institution on domestic rails within domestic cut-off times.

Why the distinction matters commercially

Consider a common complaint: “the payment took four days”. Which of the five functions consumed the four days? If it was messaging, the fix is a data or connectivity problem. If it was clearing, the answer may be a netting cycle that runs once a day by design. If it was compliance holds arising from incomplete party data in the message, the fix is upstream data quality, not a faster network. If it was conversion waiting on currency availability, no technology changes that. If it was payout arriving after a domestic cut-off, the constraint is a local operating window.

Five different diagnoses, five different remedies, one symptom. A vendor who cannot say which one they address is not offering a solution; they are offering a slogan.

Netting versus gross settlement

Clearing systems that net obligations reduce the value that must move. If a hundred instructions in both directions collapse into one net figure, only that figure needs to be settled. That is efficient in liquidity terms, and it is why netting is used widely.

The cost is exposure between cycles. Until settlement occurs, participants carry obligations to one another. Real-time gross settlement takes the opposite trade: each instruction settles individually and immediately, typically in central bank money, which removes that exposure but requires participants to hold more liquidity through the day.

Neither is better in the abstract. Systems choose different points on this trade for good reasons, and a design that criticises another for its choice is usually ignoring the constraint that produced it.

Finality is a legal property

The most consequential point, and the one most often skipped. Settlement finality is the moment a transfer becomes irrevocable and unconditional — not reversible by the sender, by a participant’s insolvency, or by the operator. It is defined by the rules of the system and by the law governing it.

A balance that updates instantly on a screen is not therefore final. A business releasing goods against a credit that is not final is carrying a risk it has probably not priced. Whenever a settlement design is presented, the question to ask is: at what point, under whose rules, and under which law, does this become irrevocable?

Applying the frame

Once the five functions are separated, comparisons become tractable. A messaging network and a settlement system are not competitors. A currency conversion service and a clearing system are not substitutes. An orchestration layer that coordinates the sequence is not a settlement system, whatever its marketing says, unless it provides finality — which requires legal standing, not software.

The frame is also a useful test of any proposition, including ours. Which of the five does it perform? Which does it depend on others to perform? If the answer is unclear, the design is unclear.

What PAPSS does, where it operates, and where private platforms may complement it

The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System is frequently invoked in market commentary and rarely described accurately. This is what it is, what it does, and what it does not attempt.

By Eko Infrastructure editorial team Published [DATE] Last updated [DATE] 10 min read Source-checked

Editorial position

Eko Infrastructure is operated by a company building in the same market. We therefore hold ourselves to a specific standard on this subject: we describe PAPSS as its operators describe it, we do not characterise its adoption without a cited figure, and we do not present any product as a replacement for it. Where we make a judgement, we mark it as a judgement.

What it is

The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System is a payment and settlement infrastructure developed with the African Export-Import Bank and African central banks. Its purpose is to enable payments between participating institutions across African markets in local currencies, with settlement arrangements involving participating central banks, rather than requiring every transaction to route through a currency and a correspondent outside the continent.

The distinguishing idea is the settlement arrangement. If two institutions in different African markets can transact with each other in their own currencies, and the resulting positions are settled through an arrangement involving their central banks, the transaction does not require the traditional chain of foreign correspondents. [CONFIRM — precise operating model, from PAPSS official documentation]

Who participates

Participation runs through central banks and, beneath them, commercial banks and payment service providers in each participating market. A business does not use PAPSS directly; it uses its bank, which may or may not be connected.

That structure has a direct consequence for anyone assessing a corridor: whether PAPSS is available for a given payment depends on whether both institutions in that specific transaction participate, not on whether their countries do. [CONFIRM — current participating central banks and institution counts, with source and date]

What we do not say about it

Commentary about PAPSS frequently asserts that adoption is negligible, or that a private-sector product is a “replacement play”. We do not publish either claim.

The first requires a figure with a source, a period and a definition of what is being counted — instructions, value, connected institutions, active corridors. Those are different measures that give different answers, and a system built through central banks scales on a timeline that is not comparable to a consumer product.

The second misdescribes the architecture. A system operating with central banks in an official capacity is not in the same category as a private orchestration layer, and framing it as a competitor tells you more about the person framing it than about the market.

Where private platforms may complement it

This is the part that requires care, because it is where our own interest lies. Stated plainly as a judgement rather than a fact:

  • Reach beyond participants. A specific corridor may involve institutions where one side is not connected. Something has to serve that transaction today.
  • Non-African counterparties. A continental system addresses payments within the continent. Trade with counterparties outside it involves different arrangements.
  • Data and reconciliation. Carrying commercial reference data end to end, and producing a single evidenced record for both sides, is a distinct problem from moving value, and it persists whichever settlement route is used.
  • Corridor-specific compliance orchestration. Screening, limits, case management and reporting tuned to one corridor sit above settlement rather than inside it.

Note what is not on that list: any suggestion that a private platform settles better, faster or more cheaply than an arrangement operating with central banks. We have no evidence for such a claim and would not publish it without any.

How to assess it for a specific corridor

  1. Are both institutions in this transaction connected?
  2. Is the currency pair supported for this direction?
  3. What is the effective timing, including domestic cut-offs at both ends?
  4. What reference data survives to the beneficiary’s statement?
  5. What does the payer’s bank actually charge for this route?

If the answers work, that is the route. A corridor proposition that cannot say when the incumbent option is the better one is not analysis.

Technology · Explainer

SWIFT is a messaging network: what that means for anyone comparing payment systems

“SWIFT takes three to five days” is one of the most repeated statements in fintech marketing, and one of the least examined. The network carries instructions. What happens to the value is a different question with a different answer for every route.

By Eko Infrastructure editorial team Published [DATE] Last updated [DATE] 9 min read Source-checked

What the network actually does

SWIFT is a cooperative that operates a secure messaging network used by financial institutions to exchange standardised instructions. When a bank sends a payment instruction over it, what travels is a message: parties, identifiers, amount, currency, charges, purpose and references.

Value does not travel over the network. Value moves through accounts the institutions hold with one another, with a shared correspondent, or with a central bank. The message tells the other institution what to do; the accounts are where it happens.

Why the “three to five days” claim fails

Once messaging and settlement are separated, the generalisation collapses. Between two banks with a direct relationship and aligned cut-offs, a payment may complete the same day. Through a chain of intermediaries, across time zones, with a compliance hold at one hop, it may take considerably longer. The determinants are the route, the currencies, the size, the time of day, the completeness of the data and the compliance outcome — not the messaging network.

Presenting a worst case as a universal property of the network is a marketing device rather than an analysis. Eko Infrastructure does not publish it, and treats its use elsewhere as a signal about the publisher.

It is also worth noting that the industry has invested substantially in tracking and speed for cross-border payments over the last decade, and that measured performance has changed as a result. Any claim about timing needs a date attached to it. [CONFIRM — current published performance data, with source and date]

What actually causes delay

  • Incomplete party data. Missing or malformed originator and beneficiary information triggers holds at downstream institutions, which is a data problem rather than a network problem.
  • Screening review. A potential sanctions match requires human disposition, and correctly so.
  • Cut-off times. An instruction arriving after a domestic cut-off waits for the next processing window, however fast it travelled.
  • Currency availability. Conversion cannot complete if the currency is not obtainable at the required size.
  • Chain length. Each additional intermediary adds its own checks, fees and windows.

Four of these five persist regardless of which messaging or settlement technology is used. That is the point that matters for anyone evaluating a proposition: the proposition has to address the causes, not the symptom.

The ISO 20022 point

The migration towards ISO 20022 messaging matters here because it targets the first cause directly. Structured party and remittance fields carry more of the data that downstream institutions need, which reduces holds and improves reconciliation. Coexistence with older formats is the normal state across markets, and the practical question for any route is which fields survive each hop.

How to compare systems honestly

A defensible comparison names the function under discussion. Comparing a messaging network to a settlement system is a category error. Comparing two settlement arrangements requires stating the settlement asset, the finality rules and the governing law. Comparing costs requires stating the size band, the currency pair, the direction and the date.

Applied to our own operator: EKORails does not replace SWIFT, because it is not a messaging network, and it does not replace a settlement system, because it does not provide finality. It is being designed as an orchestration and record layer that depends on both. Any material from us that suggests otherwise should be reported as an error.

Corridors · Method

How to read a trade corridor: a method, and what the data will not tell you

Corridor analysis usually starts with a market-size number and works down. That produces a figure nobody can act on. Starting from named participants and working up produces a description that can be tested.

By Eko Infrastructure editorial team Published [DATE] Last updated [DATE] 11 min read Source-checked

Why market-size analysis fails here

A headline like “a two-hundred-billion-dollar market” is usually built by taking a broad trade or payments aggregate, applying a share, and applying a fee assumption. Each step is defensible on its own and the product is unusable, because the aggregate includes flows that are not addressable, the share is an assumption, and the fee varies by an order of magnitude across size bands.

Worse, the number cannot be falsified. Nothing you observe on a real corridor will confirm or refute it, so it cannot guide a decision. Any market figure worth publishing shows its inputs, its assumptions and its arithmetic, and calls itself an estimate.

The method

Step 1 — Name the participants

List the institutions that actually move value on this corridor: banks on both sides, payment institutions, switches, aggregators, and the informal channels that carry real volume whether or not anyone likes it. If you cannot name them, you do not yet have a corridor description.

Step 2 — Separate the flows

Trade payments for goods, trade payments for services, intra-group treasury movement, personal remittance and institutional transfers behave differently in value, frequency, documentation and regulatory treatment. Statistics about one are not evidence about another, and conflating them is the most common error in corridor decks.

Step 3 — Build the cost stack

Origination fee, conversion margin, intermediary charges, receiving charge — then the costs that never appear on an invoice: working capital held against timing uncertainty, staff time spent reconciling unmatched credits, and delay in releasing goods. For many businesses the invisible half is larger than the visible one, which is why fee-only comparisons mislead.

Step 4 — Locate the friction

Ask where transactions actually stop. Compliance holds from incomplete party data. Currency unavailable at size. Cut-offs missed across time zones. Beneficiary details failing validation at the last hop. Reference data lost between intermediaries. Each has a different remedy, and a proposition that does not name which one it addresses is not a proposition.

Step 5 — Test the last mile

How does value reach the beneficiary domestically — through which licensed institution, on which rails, within which window? This is where corridor propositions most often fail, because it cannot be built remotely and cannot be assumed.

Step 6 — Draw the regulatory perimeter

Which activities require authorisation, in which market, and who holds it? Which reporting obligations attach, and to whom? What data must remain in-market? A design that is elegant and unlicensable is not a design.

What the data will not tell you

Being explicit about limits is part of the method, not an apology for it.

  • Corridor-level business payment volume is generally not published. Most circulating figures are estimates whose assumptions are not stated.
  • Recorded trade is not payment volume. Netting, financing, re-exports and informal channels all break the link.
  • Remittance pricing data does not describe business payments. It is carefully collected and it measures a different product.
  • Time-to-settle has no single value. It is a distribution that depends on route, size, currency, time and compliance outcome.
  • Informal flows are unmeasured by definition. Estimates differ by multiples between sources.

None of this makes corridor analysis impossible. It makes it qualitative in places, and the honest response is to say which parts are measured, which are estimated and which are judgement.
