Stop Re-Deciding What You Have Already Decided

Most broken commitments are not failures of willpower. They are decisions that were never allowed to stay decided. Here is how to settle one, and when to review it.

By
MO
Date
October 19, 2026
Category
Discipline & Decision-Making

Think of a decision you have made more than twice.

One you have already made, sincerely, more than once. To protect the first hour of the morning. To stop answering work messages after dinner. To finish the project you keep returning to. Each time you meant it. Each time it held for a few days or a few weeks, and then it quietly ended with a reasonable exception, followed by a slightly less reasonable one.

When that happens, most of us reach for the same verdict: I lack discipline. Book 2 of the GoBeyond Series, Discipline, offers a more useful diagnosis. The decision did not fail because you were weak. You kept putting it back up for a vote.

The vote you keep holding

A decision that can be reopened every evening is not really a decision. It is a proposal, and evenings are not neutral judges. The version of you doing the reconsidering is usually more tired, under more pressure and thinking about a much shorter time horizon than the version of you who made the decision.

The book describes this pattern plainly. A person makes a decision “and then permitted it to be reopened every single evening, by circumstances that were always slightly different and always slightly compelling.” That is not a failure to carry out a policy. It is a referendum on the policy, held nightly.

The three costs of reopening

Every reopened decision has a cost, and the obvious one is the least important.

You often lose. Decisions remade under fatigue tend to go differently from decisions made with a clear head.

The argument itself costs you. Even on the days you win and do the thing, you have spent attention holding your position against a case for the alternative. Arguing with yourself is not free.

You learn that your decisions are provisional. This is the cost that does the real damage. After enough rounds, a decision no longer feels like a decision when you make it. It arrives with a small internal asterisk. In the book’s words: “They have not become weak. They have accumulated evidence.”

That last line matters for anyone building something, whether it is a business, a career, a household, a ministry or a personal practice. What people call losing trust in themselves is often an accurate reading of their own track record. Rebuilding it means changing what happens at the moment of choice, not promising harder.

Why some decisions never come up for debate

Notice that you do not renegotiate most of your life. You do not decide each morning whether to get dressed. You do not weigh, at the till, whether to pay for what is in your basket. These are not triumphs of self-control. They are settled matters, and settled matters cost almost nothing, because no argument takes place.

The book names three things that settle a matter:

  • Identity. It is simply not the kind of thing you do, so the question never arises.
  • Consequence. The cost of the alternative is immediate and certain enough that no case can be made for it.
  • Structure. The choice has been removed, delegated or automated, so there is nothing left to decide.

Notice what is missing: wanting it badly enough. The things you never renegotiate are not the things you want most. They are the things placed beyond the reach of daily reconsideration.

Decide once

The book draws on an old picture of this idea. In Homer’s Odyssey, Odysseus wants to hear the Sirens without wrecking his ship. He has himself bound to the mast and tells his crew that if he begs to be released, they should tie him tighter. He knows that his future self will want out and will have excellent reasons. So he removes that future self’s authority to renegotiate, while he can still see clearly.

You already do versions of this: an alarm across the room, savings that leave your account before you see them, a colleague you told. Each one is you, in a clear hour, arranging things so that a more tired version of you has less room to bargain.

That is what deciding once means. Not deciding more firmly. Deciding, and then arranging things so the decision does not have to be won again every day.

Settled is not the same as rigid

This principle has an important limit, and the book insists on it. “Decide once, execute always” is a memorable phrase and a dangerous rule. A person who can never update a bad decision is not disciplined. They are stuck.

So be precise about what is settled:

The decision is settled. The details are not. Whether you protect the first hour of your morning is closed. Whether today that hour starts at six or seven, at home or elsewhere, in full or in a reduced form, is still yours to arrange. Adjusting the details is not reopening the decision.

The decision is settled until it is reviewed. There is a difference between reopening a commitment at the moment of action and reviewing it at a planned time when you are calm. The first is negotiation. The second is judgment.

The operative point is simple: settled means the question does not get a vote at the moment of action.

The order matters

There is a quiet detail in the first chapter of the book of Daniel that the book draws attention to. Daniel, a young man taken into training in a foreign court, first resolved not to eat from the king’s table (Daniel 1:8). Only then did he approach the official in charge and propose a respectful ten-day trial of a different diet.

The book is careful to offer this as a reading of the sequence rather than a claim about what the text sets out to teach. But the sequence is on the page. The resolve came first. The negotiation was only about how. He was firm about the decision and entirely reasonable about how to carry it out.

Most of us do it the other way round, keeping the decision open and hoping circumstances will make it for us.

One thing to try this week

Do not try to settle everything. Start with one: a commitment you have already broken more than twice, because the evidence is in that it cannot survive being decided again and again.

  1. Write it down as settled. One sentence, present tense, no conditions.
  2. Add one piece of friction. Not a reminder, because reminders inform rather than bind. Something that makes reopening the question harder: a fixed time in your calendar, a person who will ask, an arrangement that removes the choice.
  3. Set a review date. Pick a calm moment, perhaps a month from now, when you will honestly ask whether this commitment still deserves its place.

Then, when the moment of action arrives, notice the old argument starting, and let it go unanswered. The question has already been answered. Your only job now is the doing.
