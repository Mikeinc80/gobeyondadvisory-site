How to read a trade corridor: a method, and what the data will not tell you

Corridor analysis usually starts with a market-size number and works down. That produces a figure nobody can act on. Starting from named participants and working up produces a description that can be tested.

By Eko Infrastructure editorial team Published [DATE] Last updated [DATE] 11 min read Source-checked

Why market-size analysis fails here

A headline like “a two-hundred-billion-dollar market” is usually built by taking a broad trade or payments aggregate, applying a share, and applying a fee assumption. Each step is defensible on its own and the product is unusable, because the aggregate includes flows that are not addressable, the share is an assumption, and the fee varies by an order of magnitude across size bands.

Worse, the number cannot be falsified. Nothing you observe on a real corridor will confirm or refute it, so it cannot guide a decision. Any market figure worth publishing shows its inputs, its assumptions and its arithmetic, and calls itself an estimate.

The method

Step 1 — Name the participants

List the institutions that actually move value on this corridor: banks on both sides, payment institutions, switches, aggregators, and the informal channels that carry real volume whether or not anyone likes it. If you cannot name them, you do not yet have a corridor description.

Step 2 — Separate the flows

Trade payments for goods, trade payments for services, intra-group treasury movement, personal remittance and institutional transfers behave differently in value, frequency, documentation and regulatory treatment. Statistics about one are not evidence about another, and conflating them is the most common error in corridor decks.

Step 3 — Build the cost stack

Origination fee, conversion margin, intermediary charges, receiving charge — then the costs that never appear on an invoice: working capital held against timing uncertainty, staff time spent reconciling unmatched credits, and delay in releasing goods. For many businesses the invisible half is larger than the visible one, which is why fee-only comparisons mislead.

Step 4 — Locate the friction

Ask where transactions actually stop. Compliance holds from incomplete party data. Currency unavailable at size. Cut-offs missed across time zones. Beneficiary details failing validation at the last hop. Reference data lost between intermediaries. Each has a different remedy, and a proposition that does not name which one it addresses is not a proposition.

Step 5 — Test the last mile

How does value reach the beneficiary domestically — through which licensed institution, on which rails, within which window? This is where corridor propositions most often fail, because it cannot be built remotely and cannot be assumed.

Step 6 — Draw the regulatory perimeter

Which activities require authorisation, in which market, and who holds it? Which reporting obligations attach, and to whom? What data must remain in-market? A design that is elegant and unlicensable is not a design.

What the data will not tell you

Being explicit about limits is part of the method, not an apology for it.

  • Corridor-level business payment volume is generally not published. Most circulating figures are estimates whose assumptions are not stated.
  • Recorded trade is not payment volume. Netting, financing, re-exports and informal channels all break the link.
  • Remittance pricing data does not describe business payments. It is carefully collected and it measures a different product.
  • Time-to-settle has no single value. It is a distribution that depends on route, size, currency, time and compliance outcome.
  • Informal flows are unmeasured by definition. Estimates differ by multiples between sources.

None of this makes corridor analysis impossible. It makes it qualitative in places, and the honest response is to say which parts are measured, which are estimated and which are judgement.
