What PAPSS does, where it operates, and where private platforms may complement it

The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System is frequently invoked in market commentary and rarely described accurately. This is what it is, what it does, and what it does not attempt.

By Eko Infrastructure editorial team Published [DATE] Last updated [DATE] 10 min read Source-checked

Editorial position

Eko Infrastructure is operated by a company building in the same market. We therefore hold ourselves to a specific standard on this subject: we describe PAPSS as its operators describe it, we do not characterise its adoption without a cited figure, and we do not present any product as a replacement for it. Where we make a judgement, we mark it as a judgement.

What it is

The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System is a payment and settlement infrastructure developed with the African Export-Import Bank and African central banks. Its purpose is to enable payments between participating institutions across African markets in local currencies, with settlement arrangements involving participating central banks, rather than requiring every transaction to route through a currency and a correspondent outside the continent.

The distinguishing idea is the settlement arrangement. If two institutions in different African markets can transact with each other in their own currencies, and the resulting positions are settled through an arrangement involving their central banks, the transaction does not require the traditional chain of foreign correspondents. Who participates

Participation runs through central banks and, beneath them, commercial banks and payment service providers in each participating market. A business does not use PAPSS directly; it uses its bank, which may or may not be connected.

That structure has a direct consequence for anyone assessing a corridor: whether PAPSS is available for a given payment depends on whether both institutions in that specific transaction participate, not on whether their countries do. What we do not say about it

Commentary about PAPSS frequently asserts that adoption is negligible, or that a private-sector product is a “replacement play”. We do not publish either claim.

The first requires a figure with a source, a period and a definition of what is being counted — instructions, value, connected institutions, active corridors. Those are different measures that give different answers, and a system built through central banks scales on a timeline that is not comparable to a consumer product.

The second misdescribes the architecture. A system operating with central banks in an official capacity is not in the same category as a private orchestration layer, and framing it as a competitor tells you more about the person framing it than about the market.

Where private platforms may complement it

This is the part that requires care, because it is where our own interest lies. Stated plainly as a judgement rather than a fact:

  • Reach beyond participants. A specific corridor may involve institutions where one side is not connected. Something has to serve that transaction today.
  • Non-African counterparties. A continental system addresses payments within the continent. Trade with counterparties outside it involves different arrangements.
  • Data and reconciliation. Carrying commercial reference data end to end, and producing a single evidenced record for both sides, is a distinct problem from moving value, and it persists whichever settlement route is used.
  • Corridor-specific compliance orchestration. Screening, limits, case management and reporting tuned to one corridor sit above settlement rather than inside it.

Note what is not on that list: any suggestion that a private platform settles better, faster or more cheaply than an arrangement operating with central banks. We have no evidence for such a claim and would not publish it without any.

How to assess it for a specific corridor

  1. Are both institutions in this transaction connected?
  2. Is the currency pair supported for this direction?
  3. What is the effective timing, including domestic cut-offs at both ends?
  4. What reference data survives to the beneficiary’s statement?
  5. What does the payer’s bank actually charge for this route?

If the answers work, that is the route. A corridor proposition that cannot say when the incumbent option is the better one is not analysis.
