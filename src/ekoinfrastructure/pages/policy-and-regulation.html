Policy and regulation.

The regulatory architecture around cross-border payments, described as it is rather than as builders would prefer it to be. Regulation is the constraint that makes settlement infrastructure trustworthy, not an obstacle to route around.

Regulatory sandboxes

A sandbox is a supervised testing arrangement. A regulator permits a firm to test a defined proposition with a defined participant group, within limits — value caps, volume caps, a duration — and subject to reporting. The regulator retains the power to stop the test.

Four things a sandbox is not, each of which is regularly misrepresented in market communications:

  • It is not a commercial operating licence.
  • It is not permission to serve the general public.
  • It is not an endorsement, approval or recommendation of the firm by the regulator.
  • It is not a guarantee that authorisation will follow.

The distinction between applying to a sandbox, being admitted to one, and holding a licence is the single most commonly blurred line in this sector. A firm that has applied has done exactly that and nothing more.

The operator of this platform, EKORails LTD, has applied to participate in the Central Bank of Nigeria Regulatory Sandbox. Participation and all proposed pilot activities remain subject to regulatory review and approval. Its current status is published at ekorails.com.

Licensing categories

Payment activity is not one licence. Different jurisdictions define different categories, and an entity commonly needs several, or needs partners who hold them. Categories that recur across African markets include payment service provider or payment solution service provider licences; international money transfer operator authorisations; switching and processing licences; e-money issuance; and banking licences.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the question “are they licensed?” is incomplete without “for what activity, in which market?”. Second, an infrastructure layer that does not itself hold funds or convert currency may sit outside some categories entirely — but only if the boundary is genuinely maintained in the design, not merely in the marketing.

AML and CFT expectations

The baseline is set internationally by the FATF Recommendations and implemented by national law and regulator guidance. In practice that means a documented risk assessment; customer and business due diligence proportionate to risk; sanctions screening; ongoing transaction monitoring; suspicious activity reporting; record keeping; a named responsible officer; training; and independent testing.

For cross-border payments the requirement that most often bites is the transmission of complete originator and beneficiary information with the payment. Chains that lose that data create obligations for every downstream institution and produce the compliance holds that customers experience as unexplained delay.

Data protection

Payment data is personal data. Nigeria’s Data Protection Act 2023 established a statutory regime and a commission with supervisory powers; other markets in the region have their own regimes at varying stages of implementation, and European or UK rules may also apply depending on where an entity or its processors are established.

For corridor design this affects lawful basis, minimisation, retention, cross-border transfer mechanisms and the handling of data subject rights — and it affects them in both markets of the corridor simultaneously, which is the part most often underestimated.

Regional and continental policy

Continental initiatives shape what is possible at corridor level. The African Continental Free Trade Area addresses trade rules; the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System addresses payment and settlement between participating institutions in local currencies; regional economic communities pursue their own integration programmes.

These are not marketing backdrops. They determine which routes exist, which currencies can be settled in, and which institutions can reach each other — and a corridor proposition that does not engage with them seriously is unlikely to survive contact with a supervisor.

How to read a firm’s regulatory claims

Five questions

  1. Which legal entity holds the status, and is it the entity you would contract with?
  2. Is the status an application, an admission to a test, or an authorisation?
  3. Which activities does it cover, in which market?
  4. Which functions are performed by licensed partners rather than by the firm?
  5. Can the firm show you the document, with a date?

These questions apply to the operator of this platform exactly as they apply to anyone else.
