Technology.

Standards, architecture and evidence. The engineering questions that determine whether a settlement design can actually be supervised.

Messaging standards

Payment messages carry more than an amount. They carry the parties, their identifiers, the purpose, the charges and the references that let a beneficiary work out what the payment is for. When those fields are lost or truncated between systems, the consequences appear later as compliance holds and manual reconciliation.

ISO 20022 is the standard the industry has converged on, and its value is precisely that it carries structured, extensible party and remittance data rather than free text. Migration across markets and systems has been staged over years, and coexistence with older formats is the normal condition rather than an exception.

For anyone designing a corridor product, the practical question is not whether to support ISO 20022 but which fields survive each hop on the actual route, and what happens to a payment when one of them does not. [CONFIRM — current migration status by market and system, with source]

Compliance architecture

Compliance is an architectural property, not a department. Three design decisions determine whether a system can be supervised.

  • Where controls run. Controls enforced before routing prevent bad instructions from entering the chain. Controls applied after the fact produce reports about payments that already left.
  • What is recorded. Recording an outcome is not enough. Recording the rule version, the input data and the decision-maker is what allows a supervisor to ask about a past date and get an answer.
  • Who owns the decision. In a chain of institutions, exactly one party must own each regulated decision. Designs that leave this ambiguous fail their first serious review.

Transaction monitoring

Monitoring compares activity against an expected pattern and raises alerts on deviation. Its engineering difficulties are well known: rules produce large numbers of false positives; thresholds that are too tight bury analysts and too loose miss activity; and tuning without versioning destroys the ability to explain past behaviour.

Two claims should always be treated sceptically. First, that a monitoring system detects all illicit activity — none does. Second, that machine learning removes the need for explainability — supervisors ask why an alert was or was not raised, and a model that cannot answer creates a regulatory problem rather than solving one.

Auditability

An auditable system can answer, for any transaction and any date: what was known, what rule applied, who decided, when, and what happened next. That requires append-only records, versioned rules, time stamps from a trusted source, and retention for the longest applicable period across the markets involved.

Auditability is also what makes disputes tractable. When two institutions disagree about a payment, the party that can reconstruct the sequence with evidence resolves the matter; the party that cannot absorbs the loss.

What distributed ledger designs change

Shared-ledger architectures change one thing genuinely: participants can read from a common record rather than reconciling separate ones. That is a real reduction in a real cost.

Several things they do not change. Somebody must still convert currency and be compensated for it. Somebody must still hold customer funds under a licence. Legal finality still depends on the law and the system rules, not on the data structure. Compliance obligations still attach to the institutions in the chain. And the last mile still runs through a licensed institution on domestic rails.

Eko Infrastructure takes no position for or against these designs. The useful question is always which of the five payment functions a proposal addresses, and what it leaves untouched.

Data sovereignty

Several markets have introduced requirements about where payment and personal data is stored and processed, and about the conditions for transferring it abroad. For corridor designs this is not a hosting preference; it determines architecture, because both markets’ requirements apply at once and can conflict.

The practical questions are: which data must remain in-market, which may be transferred under which mechanism, who can access it from where, and how that is evidenced. [CONFIRM — current residency requirements by market, with source and date]
